5. that the raw columns of the CSV are the natural coordinates of the process, rather than
   one arbitrary projection of it.

Each of these is worth attacking on its own. The order below goes from the most abstract
(what kind of object are we looking at) to the most concrete (which transformations and
which mechanisms to test), and ends with the questions that remain genuinely open.

A recurring device: for each theoretical claim I state **what observation would falsify
it** on this venue. A theory of the market that cannot be wrong about Nobitex USDT/IRT is

## 1. What kind of object is "the market"?

### 1.1 Three senses of "predictable"

Let \(X_{t+1}\) be the next transition (flow pair, mid change, spread change, …) and
\(\mathcal{F}_t\) the information available before it. "Is the market predictable?" has at
least three inequivalent meanings, and much confusion comes from sliding between them.

**(P1) Mean predictability.** \(\mathbb{E}[X_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] \neq \mathbb{E}[X_{t+1}]\)
for a non-null set of histories. This is the efficient-market question. For the mid it
asks whether \(\mathbb{E}[\Delta M_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\) is anything other than a
constant. The data says *yes, weakly*: the lag-1 autocorrelation of \(\Delta M\) is
\(-0.238\). An AR(1) predictor of the mean captures \(R^2 = \rho_1^2 \approx 0.057\) of the
variance. That is small in variance terms but **it is not zero**, and for a market maker
it is precisely the bid–ask bounce they harvest (see §5.3).

**(P2) Distributional predictability.** \(P(X_{t+1} \in \cdot \mid \mathcal{F}_t)\) varies
with the history even when its mean does not. This is the volatility question. The data
says *yes, strongly*: rolling one-hour variance of \(\Delta M\) spans a ratio of 52×, and
\(\mathrm{ACF}(|\Delta M|)\) decays over hours, not seconds. A process can be a
martingale (P1 fails) and still be highly predictable in the sense of P2.

**(P3) Entropy predictability.** \(H(X_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t) < H(X_{t+1})\). This is the
information-theoretic question and is the one the log score measures. P1 and P2 both
imply P3, but P3 is the *only* one of the three that is directly the quantity we are
scored on. The mutual information \(I(X_{t+1}; \mathcal{F}_t) = H(X_{t+1}) - H(X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_t)\)
is the total prize in nats per step; §2 decomposes it.

The market-maker literature's "efficient markets" slogan concerns P1 only. Nothing about
P1 failing or holding tells us whether P2 or P3 are worth pursuing. On this venue P1 is
weakly present, P2 is strongly present, and P3 is what we must quantify.

### 1.2 Deterministic, stochastic, or neither

A time series can be (a) a deterministic dynamical system observed with noise, (b) a
genA time series can be (a) a deterministic dynamical system observed through noise, (b) a
genuinely stochastic process — an innovation sequence pushed through a filter — or
(c) an *open system*: the aggregate of a population of agents whose composition,
constraints, and information change over time, so that no fixed law generates the data at
all.

**Against (a).** Tests for low-dimensional determinism (false nearest neighbours,
correlation dimension, recurrence quantification) need long, clean, stationary series.
With 8 700 heavy-tailed points they cannot separate (a) from (b), and there is a prior
argument that makes the attempt pointless: a 30 s bucket sums the actions of many agents
acting on partly private information. Aggregation over many weakly dependent decisions is
the canonical *generator* of stochastic description. We should treat the stochastic view
not as a fallback but as the natural coarse-grained law of a many-agent system.

**A structural consequence.** If a bucket is a sum of a *random number* of trades of
*random size*, its law is a compound distribution, and compound distributions produce
heavy tails from *mixing alone* — even when every individual trade is well-behaved. Excess
kurtosis of 7.18 in \(\Delta M\) is therefore *not* evidence of a heavy-tailed innovation;
it is equally consistent with a Gaussian innovation whose scale is randomised bucket to
bucket. These two hypotheses lead to different models (Student-t innovation vs. Gaussian
scale mixture / stochastic volatility) and they are distinguishable:

> **Test 1.2.a.** Standardise \(\Delta M_t\) by a *strictly causal* local scale
> \(\hat\sigma_{t-1}\) (e.g. EWMA of \(|\Delta M|\) with half-life ~30 min). If the
> standardised residual has excess kurtosis near 0 and \(\mathrm{ACF}(|z|)\) near 0, the
> tails are *generated by volatility mixing* and the right innovation is Gaussian on a
> time-varying scale. If kurtosis stays ≫ 0, there is a heavy-tailed innovation *on top of*
> the mixing and Student-t / NIG is warranted. The roadmap assumed the second without
> running the first. Falsifiable, cheap, decisive.

**On (c).** The open-system view is the one that matters most and is the hardest to
formalise. It says: the "process" is not a fixed measure \(P\) on path space but a
sequence \(P_1, P_2, \dots\) indexed by the agent population, and the population is not
observed. §3 takes this seriously instead of treating it as "non-stationarity".

### 1.3 The bucket is a coarse-graining, not the process

At the exchange the process is a *marked point process* — arrivals \(\tau_i\) with marks
(side, size, aggressor, resulting book state) — plus a pure-jump process for the book.
The 30 s bucket integrates over it and destroys: order of events within the bucket,
inter-arrival times, which side moved first, and whether a mid change *preceded* or
*followed* the flow that shares its bucket.

This matters for the one live signal in the data. The backward correlation
\(\mathrm{corr}(\text{net-flow}_{t-1}, \Delta M_t) = 0.171\) is presented as "prior bucket's
flow moves current bucket's mid." An alternative reading: a trade arriving at second 29 of
bucket \(t-1\) walks the book, and the mid update is *recorded* in bucket \(t\). The
correlation is then the shadow of **within-bucket mechanics smeared across an arbitrary
boundary**, not lagged predictability.

> **Test 1.3.a.** Re-bucket the same events at 10 s, 30 s, 60 s, 120 s. If the lag-1
> backward correlation *grows* as buckets shrink and *shrinks* toward 0 as they widen, it
> is a boundary artefact of mechanical impact. If it is scale-stable, it is genuine lagged
> price adjustment (slow incorporation of order-flow information — a real inefficiency).
> Only the second is a *predictive* asset; the first is already inside the simulator.

The same logic applies to the Roll bounce. \(\rho_1 = -0.238\) at 30 s is the mixed result
of tick-level bounce and genuine mean-reversion. Under pure bounce the *variance ratio*
\(\mathrm{VR}(k) = \mathrm{Var}(\Delta_k M)/(k\,\mathrm{Var}(\Delta_1 M))\) rises from
\(1 + 2\rho_1 \approx 0.52\) at \(k=1\) toward 1 and flattens; under genuine mean
reversion it keeps falling. This has not been computed and should be (§5.3).

### 1.4 The state-space view

Everything above converges on one formal picture: an unobserved state \(Z_t\) (efficient
price, liquidity regime, agent composition, reference-venue deviation) driving observed
projections \(x_t = g(Z_t) + \text{noise}\). Prediction is *filtering*, and the questions
"is it predictable?" and "is it stationary?" both become questions about \(Z_t\): how many
dimensions does it have, how fast does it move, and how much of it is identifiable from
\(x_t\)? §2 measures the answer in nats; §3 asks what happens when \(Z_t\) has no
stationary law.

## 2. Decomposing uncertainty: what the floor is made of

The roadmap treats the entropy rate \(h\) as "the floor" and the gap \(\bar\ell - h\) as
"the prize." That is too coarse. The log loss we pay per step splits into four
qualitatively different pieces, and each one calls for a different remedy — or no remedy.

### 2.1 The four-way decomposition

Fix the *recorded* information \(\mathcal{G}_t = \sigma(x_{0:t})\) (the CSV filtration) and
the *full* information \(\mathcal{F}_t \supseteq \mathcal{G}_t\) (everything the venue and
the bot could in principle observe: full book, reference venue, event ordering, our own
quotes). Let \(P\) be the true transition law and \(Q_t\) our model. Then, in expectation,

\[
\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[-\log Q_t(X_{t+1})]}_{\text{what we pay}}
= \underbrace{H(X_{t+1}\mid \mathcal{F}_t)}_{\text{(A) aleatoric}}
+ \underbrace{I(X_{t+1};\mathcal{F}_t \mid \mathcal{G}_t)}_{\text{(B) unrecorded information}}
+ \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\bigl(P(\cdot\mid\mathcal{G}_t)\,\|\,Q_t^{\ast}\bigr)}_{\text{(C) misspecification}}
+ \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\bigl(Q_t^{\ast}\,\|\,Q_t\bigr)}_{\text{(D) estimation}}
\]

where \(Q_t^{\ast}\) is the best member of our model class given \(\mathcal{G}_t\). (The
last two terms are not literally additive in general; they are for well-specified
exponential families and are a usable approximation otherwise.)

- **(A)** is the true intrinsic uncertainty of the venue. No model, no data, no compute
  removes it. It is the *only* thing that deserves the word "irreducible."
- **(B)** is uncertainty that *looks* intrinsic from the CSV but is not. It is removed by
  recording more — full depth, reference price, event order — not by modelling better.
- **(C)** is removed by changing the model class (e.g. Gaussian → scale mixture).
- **(D)** is removed by more data *or* a better update law, and in a non-stationary
  world it never reaches zero (§3).

The critical observation: **a lab that only has the CSV cannot distinguish (A) from
(B).** Every "entropy floor" estimated from the CSV is a floor for (A)+(B), and the
roadmap's language of "irreducible" silently absorbed (B) into (A). That is the single
largest blind spot in the current programme, because (B) is the piece that is cheapest to
remove and requires no modelling cleverness at all.

### 2.2 Bounding (B) without the full book

We cannot compute \(I(X_{t+1};\mathcal{F}_t\mid\mathcal{G}_t)\) from the CSV. But we can
bound it from below by adding *one* extra column at a time and measuring the drop in
conditional entropy. This is the **predictive-information ladder**:

\[
\Delta_k = H(X_{t+1}\mid \mathcal{G}_t) - H(X_{t+1}\mid \mathcal{G}_t, W^{(k)}_t),
\]

for candidate \(W^{(k)}\): reference-venue price, deviation of local mid from reference,
book depth at 2–5 ticks, queue ahead of our quote, event order within the last bucket.
Each \(\Delta_k \ge 0\), each is estimable by the same prequential machinery already in
`core/walk_forward.py`, and their sum is a lower bound on (B). If the first two rungs
(reference and deviation) alone yield \(\Delta > 0.1\) nats on the price factor, the venue
is an *echo* and the modelling question changes entirely (§5).

> **Test 2.2.a.** Join `mm_events` reference-venue mid to the 30 s grid. Compute
> prequential NLL of the *same* price model with and without \(\text{ref-dev}_t = M_t -
> M^{\text{ref}}_t\) as a conditioning variable. The difference *is* \(\Delta_{\text{ref}}\).
> Cost: one join, one column, zero new models.

### 2.3 Is (A) measurable from 8 700 samples?

Honest answer: **not as a number, only as an interval — and the interval is wide.**

**Why point estimation fails.** For a continuous heavy-tailed variable, the differential
entropy is dominated by the tail, the tail is the least-sampled region, and every
plug-in estimator (kernel, k-NN, Vasicek) is biased in the direction of *under*-estimating
uncertainty in the tail. With \(\alpha \in (3,5)\) and \(n = 8\,700\), the top 0.5 % of
\(|\Delta M|\) is ~43 points. The contribution of that region to \(H\) is a large fraction
of the total and has sampling error of order \(1/\sqrt{43}\). Any entropy-rate figure
quoted to two decimals is fiction.

**Why sequential estimation is worse.** Entropy *rate* (conditional on history) needs the
joint law over \((X_{t+1}, \mathcal{G}_t)\), and the curse of dimensionality bites
immediately. Lempel–Ziv and context-tree-weighting estimators are consistent for
stationary ergodic *finite-alphabet* sources; they require symbolisation (which destroys
the continuous tail we care about), converge at rate \(\log\log n / \log n\) (useless at
\(n \sim 10^4\)), and assume stationarity (§3 argues we do not have it).

**What we can do instead.** Three honest moves:

1. **Discrete factors first.** The zero/non-zero indicators for buy, sell, and \(\Delta M\)
   are binary; their conditional entropies *are* estimable to ±0.01 nats with this
   sample. Zero-flow rate 4.6 %, zero-buy 13.8 %, zero-sell 17.0 %, zero-mid-change
   14.6 % — the marginal entropies of these four bits sum to roughly 1.6 nats and are a
   non-trivial part of the total. Whether history compresses them is measurable *now*.

2. **Upper bound (A) by the best model we can build; lower bound it by the best
   model we can build on augmented information.** The floor is squeezed between the
   two. As (B) is drained by recording more, the upper bound falls and the two meet.
   This is the only convergent procedure; it replaces "estimate \(h\)" with "drive the
   bounds together."

3. **Bound the remaining prize by the variance-explained ceiling.** For the price
   factor, if \(\mathrm{Var}(\mathbb{E}[\Delta M\mid\mathcal{F}_t]) / \mathrm{Var}(\Delta M)
   \le r\), then under a Gaussian approximation the mean-prediction gain is at most
   \(-\tfrac12\log(1-r)\) nats. With \(r \approx 0.06\) (the Roll term) this is
   **≈ 0.03 nats**. The *scale* prediction gain is at most
   \(\tfrac12\,\mathbb{E}[\log \bar\sigma^2/\sigma_t^2]\), the Jensen gap of the
   volatility path; with a 52× range in hourly variance this is plausibly **0.2–0.4
   nats**. So, before building anything: on the price factor the volatility channel is
   worth an order of magnitude more than the mean channel. That ranking is *robust to
   the model class*.

### 2.4 Epistemic uncertainty about the *model*, not the parameter

Term (D) is usually treated as parameter uncertainty inside a fixed class. But the
data audit found that \(\sigma_{\text{buy}}\) starves to \(10^{-6}\) and the price
parameters were never recorded. Those are not estimation errors; they are *the model
being confidently wrong about its own state*. The proper object for epistemic uncertainty
in an online setting is not \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta)\) but the **posterior over
model instances**, and its correct expression is a Bayesian mixture over experts
(Hedge/exponential weights), whose log score is provably within \(\log K / T\) of the best
expert *in hindsight* — and, more relevantly here, whose *fixed-share* variant tracks the
best *sequence* of experts. The price of that guarantee is a per-step cost proportional
to the switching rate; §3.4 estimates what the venue's switching rate actually is.

### 2.5 A note on "reducing entropy of possible futures"

The user's phrasing — *could we reduce the entropy of possible futures?* — deserves a
precise answer. We cannot reduce \(H(X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_t)\); that is term (A). What we
can do is (i) reduce (B) by observing more, (ii) reduce (C)+(D) by modelling better, and
(iii) — the option no prediction layer offers — **change the process** by acting on it.
A market maker who quotes tighter *changes* the flow it receives and the mid it observes.
The relevant entropy for the controller is \(H(X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_t,\mathrm{do}(A_t))\),
and it is a *function of the action*. Some actions make the future less uncertain (e.g.
posting deep, joining the queue) at the cost of expected profit; the trade-off is a
control problem, not a modelling problem. §6 returns to it.

## 3. Non-stationarity as a structural fact, not a nuisance

"The data is non-stationary" is usually said to excuse a model and then immediately
ignored. Here it is taken as the central fact about the object, and given enough
structure that it can be *measured* and *modelled* instead of merely tolerated.

### 3.1 Five things "non-stationary" can mean

For the transition law \(P_t(\cdot\mid\mathcal{F}_t)\), non-stationarity can enter as:

| Type | Formal shape | Fingerprint in data | Remedy class |
|---|---|---|---|
| **N1 unit root in level** | \(M_t\) integrated; \(\Delta M_t\) stationary | trivially present — we already model \(\Delta M\), not \(M\) | difference (done) |
| **N2 slow drift of conditional moments** | \(\theta_t\) a smooth path, \(\|\theta_{t+1}-\theta_t\|\) small | ACF(\(|\Delta M|\)) decaying over hours; spread ACF(1)=0.52 | tracking estimator (EWMA, Kalman on \(\theta\)) |
| **N3 abrupt regime switches** | \(\theta_t\) piecewise constant, jumps at random times | 52× range of hourly variance; wide-spread episodes with P99 = 394 IRT | change-point / fixed-share / HMM |
| **N4 deterministic periodicity** | \(\theta_t = \theta(\tau_t) + \epsilon_t\), \(\tau_t\) time-of-day | *not yet tested* on this extract | seasonal conditioning |
| **N5 population drift** | the *map* from \(\mathcal{F}_t\) to \(P_t\) changes, not just its parameters | no fixed model class wins for long; expert weights rotate | model-class mixing, re-specification |

N1 is trivial. N2 and N3 are what the roadmap's EWMA addresses, but with a single
half-life that cannot be right for both. N4 is cheap to test and nobody has. N5 is the
one that a "dynamic online model" must ultimately face, and it is the one for which no
parameter-updating scheme is sufficient — because it is the *model*, not the parameter,
that expires.

> **Test 3.1.a (N4).** Bin the four days by hour-of-day (UTC+3:30 local). Compute per-bin
> mean of \(|\Delta M|\), zero-flow rate, and total flow. If the between-bin variance is a
> substantial fraction of the total variance of the hourly means, a time-of-day term is a
> *free* reduction of (D): it costs no memory and never goes stale. With only four days
> the estimate is noisy (4 samples per bin) but the Postgres history removes that limit.

### 3.2 The entropy rate of a non-stationary process is undefined — so what replaces it?

For a stationary ergodic process, \(h = \lim_n H(X_n\mid X_{0:n-1})\) exists and is the
floor. For N2–N5 the limit does not exist. Two replacements are honest:

**Local entropy rate.** \(h_t = H(X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_t)\) as a *path*. It is what the
market maker actually faces at time \(t\); its time-average over an epoch is the epoch's
floor. The relevant question is no longer "what is \(h\)?" but "how fast does \(h_t\) move,
and can we track it faster than it moves?" — which is a ratio of two time-scales, not a
number.

**Regret against a comparator sequence.** Fix a comparator class \(\mathcal{C}\) of
parameter *paths* \(\{\theta_t\}\) with at most \(m\) switches (or total variation
\(\le V\)). The tracking regret of an online learner is
\[
R_T(\mathcal{C}) = \sum_{t=1}^{T}-\log Q_t(x_{t+1}) - \min_{\{\theta_t\}\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}-\log Q(x_{t+1}\mid\theta_t).
\]
For fixed-share over \(K\) experts the bound is \(O\bigl(m\log(KT)\bigr)\); for
strongly-convex losses with a total-variation budget it is \(O(\sqrt{VT})\) or better.
These bounds are *the* theoretical statement of what "online" buys: the per-step cost of
tracking is \(m\log(KT)/T\), which vanishes if switches are rare relative to \(T\) and
does not if they are not.

This reframes the whole programme. The quantity to estimate from data is not \(h\) but
**the switching rate \(m/T\)** (or the variation budget \(V/T\)). If \(m/T\) is small, EWMA
tracking with a well-chosen half-life is nearly optimal and the roadmap's prescription
stands. If \(m/T\) is large, no single-half-life tracker can be close to the floor, and
the architecture must change to a mixture of trackers at different half-lives — the
cost of which is bounded by the regret theorem above.

### 3.3 Two time-scales, and their ratio

Define \(\tau_{\text{drift}}\) as the time over which \(\theta_t\) moves by one standard
error of its estimate, and \(\tau_{\text{learn}}\) as the number of steps the estimator
needs to reach that standard error. Tracking is possible iff
\(\tau_{\text{learn}} \ll \tau_{\text{drift}}\).

From the audit we can rough out both for the price scale:

- ACF(\(|\Delta M|\)) drops from 0.229 at lag 1 to 0.066 at lag 20 to 0.021 at lag 60
  — an effective memory of order **10–30 minutes**. That is \(\tau_{\text{drift}}\) for
  the volatility state.
- A scale estimator from \(n\) heavy-tailed samples (\(\alpha\approx 4\)) has relative
  standard error roughly \(\sqrt{\kappa/n}\) with \(\kappa\) the kurtosis-driven inflation
  (\(\approx 3\)–\(4\) here); to reach 20 % relative error needs \(n \approx 75\)–\(100\)
  buckets, i.e. **40–50 minutes**. That is \(\tau_{\text{learn}}\).

**\(\tau_{\text{learn}} > \tau_{\text{drift}}\).** The volatility state moves faster than
we can estimate it from mid changes alone. This is the mathematical statement of a
familiar frustration: the EWMA is always late. Three responses, in increasing ambition:

1. **Use more informative observations of the same state.** \(|\Delta M|\) is a poor
   measurement of \(\sigma_t\) — it is a single noisy draw. Intra-bucket range (high−low
   of the mid within 30 s), trade count, and spread are all *also* measurements of the
   same latent scale, each with its own noise. Combining them is a multi-sensor filtering
   problem, and it cuts \(\tau_{\text{learn}}\) by roughly the number of near-independent
   sensors. The CSV has `trade_count` and `spread`; the range needs Postgres.
2. **Put a prior on where \(\sigma_t\) goes.** If volatility mean-reverts (it does, on
   every venue ever studied) then the filter should shrink toward the long-run level,
   not toward the last observation. A log-AR(1) state on \(\log\sigma_t\) with a Kalman
   or particle update is the minimal such filter; EWMA is the special case with no
   mean reversion, which is why it drifts into \(10^{-6}\) after quiet spells.
3. **Accept that some of the volatility path is unpredictable and price it.** If the
   innovation to \(\log\sigma_t\) has variance \(q\), the filter's stationary error is
   bounded below by a function of \(q\), and that residual *is* part of (A) for the
   price factor. It should appear in the predictive as extra kurtosis — which is exactly
   why Student-t tails "work" empirically even when the innovation is Gaussian.

The same computation for the flow intensity gives the opposite ordering: total-flow
ACF(1) is 0.287 with fast decay, so its state moves at the *bucket* scale, and a
\(\tau_{\text{learn}}\) of tens of buckets is hopeless. For flow, the state is
essentially *not trackable* from its own history at 30 s, and the right model is a
mixing distribution with the state *integrated out* (a compound / doubly-stochastic
Poisson with a random intensity) rather than a tracker of the intensity. This is a
concrete, non-obvious consequence of the two-time-scale argument, and it contradicts
the naive extension of EWMA-on-everything.

### 3.4 Estimating the switching rate

Nothing above is actionable until \(m/T\) is measured. Three estimators, from cheap to
principled:

- **Half-life sweep as a diagnostic, not a search.** Run the *same* model with half-lives
  {5, 15, 30, 60, 120, 240} min and plot prequential NLL against half-life. A single
  interior minimum ⇒ N2 dominates and one tracker suffices. A flat or multi-modal curve
  ⇒ N3/N5, and the mixture is needed. (This is a grid — but its purpose is to read off a
  property of the *process*, not to tune the *model*.)
- **BOCPD run-length posterior.** Bayesian online change-point detection on the scale
  factor yields, at every step, a posterior over the time since the last change. Its
  mean run-length is a direct estimate of \(T/m\); its distribution tells us whether
  switches are Poisson (memoryless) or clustered.
- **Expert-weight entropy.** Run fixed-share over the half-life experts and record the
  entropy of the weight vector over time. Persistent low entropy ⇒ one expert wins ⇒
  stationary enough. High entropy or frequent rotation ⇒ the process is switching at
  the resolution of the expert set.

The four-day extract can support the first two. The third is a by-product of the
architecture the roadmap already recommends and costs nothing extra.

### 3.5 Population drift (N5) and the limits of any fixed model class

N2–N4 are within the reach of parameter tracking. N5 is not. If the *mechanism* that maps
history to next-transition changes — a large participant arrives, the venue changes its
matching rules, a reference feed goes down, an external event alters the composition of
flow — then the best model in any fixed class is a different *function*, not a different
point. There is no update law for that; there is only detection and re-specification.

The honest theoretical position is that the model lab should be built to **detect** N5
rather than to survive it. The detector is the residual structure itself: if the PIT
histogram of the best-scoring expert goes non-uniform in a way that persists, or if a
conditional-independence assumption that held (flow ⊥ next-mid given history) starts to
fail, the class has expired. The lab should log these as first-class outputs, alongside
NLL, with the same walk-forward discipline. A model that reports "I am no longer the
right model" is worth more to a market maker than one that reports a slightly lower
score.

## 4. Coordinates: which transformations of the raw data are principled

The CSV's columns (`mid`, `spread`, `buy_volume`, `sell_volume`, …) are one projection of
the book. A transformation is *principled* if it either (i) removes a known artefact so the
remainder is closer to the latent state, (ii) makes a symmetry of the process explicit, or
(iii) separates components that have different time-scales or different innovation laws.
Anything else is feature engineering by habit. Each candidate below is tagged with its
justification and the test that would show it earns its place.

### 4.1 Price coordinates

**Mid → efficient-price + bounce (i).** Write \(M_t = P_t + \eta_t\), with \(P_t\) a
martingale and \(\eta_t\) a bounded, mean-zero bid–ask component. The Roll decomposition
identifies the two from autocovariances alone:
\[
\sigma^2_\eta \approx -\gamma_1,\qquad \sigma^2_{\Delta P} \approx \gamma_0 + 2\gamma_1,
\]
where \(\gamma_k = \mathrm{Cov}(\Delta M_t, \Delta M_{t-k})\). With \(\rho_1 = -0.238\) and
\(\gamma_0 = 65.7^2\): \(\sigma_\eta \approx 32\) IRT and \(\sigma_{\Delta P}\approx 47\) IRT per
30 s. So roughly **half of the observed mid variance is bounce, half is efficient-price
innovation.** This is the single most important transformation in the document: the two
components have *different* predictability (bounce is P1-predictable and mean-reverting;
\(\Delta P\) is a martingale) and *different* economic meaning (bounce is the maker's
spread capture; \(\Delta P\) is adverse selection). Modelling their sum with one
innovation law forces one distribution to describe two mechanisms.

> **Test 4.1.a.** Compute \(\gamma_1,\gamma_2,\gamma_3\). Pure Roll ⇒ \(\gamma_2 \approx
> \gamma_3 \approx 0\) (audit: \(\rho_2 = -0.002\), consistent). Then fit the
> state-space form \(M_t = P_t + \eta_t\) by Kalman and check whether the filtered
> \(\Delta \hat P_t\) has ACF(1) ≈ 0 and whether \(\hat\eta_t\) is bounded by ± spread/2.
> If it is, the decomposition is *identified*, not just fitted.

**Microprice (i, partial).** \(M^{\mu}_t = \frac{q^a M^b + q^b M^a}{q^a+q^b}\) uses top-level
imbalance to move the estimate toward the side about to be hit. It is a *cheap* estimate
of \(P_t\) that uses the book instead of history. Test: is \(\mathrm{ACF}_1(\Delta M^\mu)\)
closer to 0 than \(-0.238\)? If yes, imbalance carries part of \(\eta_t\) and the two
estimates of \(P_t\) should be fused, not chosen between.

**Tick-normalised and spread-normalised returns (ii).** \(\Delta M / \text{tick}\) makes the
lattice explicit — 14.6 % exact zeros is a lattice fact, not a continuous-density fact,
and a continuous innovation law assigns it probability zero. \(\Delta M / S_{t-1}\) makes the
scaling symmetry explicit: if wide-spread regimes have proportionally wider mid moves,
the normalised series is closer to stationary. Test: compare the 52× range of hourly
variance for raw vs. spread-normalised \(\Delta M\). A large reduction means *spread is
the volatility state* and should be conditioned on, not predicted.

**Reference deviation (iii).** \(D_t = M_t - M^{\text{ref}}_t\). If the venue is an echo
(§5), \(D_t\) is a stationary, mean-reverting series with a *fast* time-scale, and \(\Delta
M^{\text{ref}}\) is the martingale part. This transformation, if it applies, dominates
everything else in this section, because it converts term (B) of §2.1 into a recorded
variable. It needs the Postgres join.

### 4.2 Flow coordinates

**Volume → (count, mean size) (iii).** \(V^+_t = \sum_{i=1}^{N^+_t} s_i\). Count and size
have different laws (count: over-dispersed integer, plausibly negative-binomial; size:
heavy-tailed positive, plausibly log-normal or Pareto-mixed), different memories, and
different dependence on the book. The CSV has `trade_count` but not per-side counts;
Postgres does. A hurdle-lognormal on the *sum* conflates a zero-count event with a
small-size event, which is precisely why the EWMA on \(\sigma_{\text{buy}}\) starves.

**Log-flow with a lattice at zero (i).** The zero atom is a count event, not a volume
event. Once counts are separated, the positive part can be modelled on the log scale
without a hurdle at all.

**Net vs. gross, and the sign process (ii).** \((V^+, V^-)\) ↦ \((V^+ + V^-,\;(V^+ - V^-)/(V^+ + V^-))\).
Total flow is the activity level (fast, scale-mixing); signed imbalance is the direction
(the *only* part that could carry P1 information about the mid). Audit: corr(buy, sell) =
0.148 and corr(\(|\text{net}|, |\Delta M|\)) = 0.062 — the direction is nearly decoupled from
the level, so the factorisation is close to exact and each factor gets its own law.

### 4.3 Time coordinates

**Clock time → activity time (ii).** Subordinate the process to cumulative trade count or
cumulative volume. Under the mixture-of-distributions hypothesis, \(\Delta P\) in
activity time is closer to Gaussian with constant variance; the heavy tails and vol
clustering in clock time are the *image* of a random activity clock. Test 1.2.a is the
same test in different clothes: if standardising by a causal scale removes the kurtosis,
so will subordination, and the two views agree. If neither works, there is a genuinely
heavy-tailed innovation and it belongs in the model.

**Event time → bucket boundary alignment (i).** Test 1.3.a (multi-scale re-bucketing)
decides whether the lag-1 flow → price signature is boundary smearing. If it is,
either shift the bucket boundary to *trade-aligned* windows, or record the within-bucket
event order so the impact is attributed to the bucket in which it occurred.

### 4.4 What a transformation is *not*

A transformation does not create information; it changes which model class can use it.
The ladder in §2.2 measures information; the tests here measure whether a given
coordinate change lets a *simpler* class reach the same score. That is the criterion:
a transformation earns its place when it lowers term (C) without raising (D).

## 5. What flow–price independence says about who sets the price

The audit's sharpest finding is the pair
\(\mathrm{corr}(\text{net-flow}_t, \Delta M_{t+1}) = 0.011\) and
\(\mathrm{corr}(\text{net-flow}_{t-1}, \Delta M_t) = 0.171\), plus the near-zero
contemporaneous coupling \(\mathrm{corr}(|\text{net}|,|\Delta M|) = 0.062\). The roadmap
recorded these as statistics. They are, first, a statement about the *institution*.

### 5.1 Three price-formation mechanisms, and their signatures

In a textbook limit-order market, price is *discovered* by order flow: informed traders
hit one side, the book thins, the mid moves, and flow Granger-causes price. Kyle, Glosten–
Milgrom, and the Hasbrouck VAR all predict a persistent, positive, *forward*
flow → price impact with a permanent component. That is mechanism **(M1) endogenous
discovery**. Its signature: \(\mathrm{corr}(\text{net}_t,\Delta M_{t+k}) > 0\) for several
\(k\), and impulse responses that do not fully revert.

Mechanism **(M2) exogenous anchoring**: the price on this venue is a noisy, lagged copy
of a price formed elsewhere (a global USDT/fiat reference, an informal FX rate, a
dominant dealer's quote). Local flow is *absorbed* by whoever anchors — a market maker
with a reference feed — and moves the mid only transiently, through mechanical
book-walking that reverts once the anchor re-quotes. Signature: forward flow → price
correlation ≈ 0; lag-1 backward correlation > 0 that *reverts* (negative at lag 2 in the
impulse response); and, if the reference is observable, \(D_t = M_t - M^{\text{ref}}_t\)
stationary with fast mean reversion.

Mechanism **(M3) dealer-set price**: one or few participants post both sides at a
spread around a price *they* choose, with size that overwhelms retail flow. Signature:
best-quote quantities that are large, persistent, and asymmetric (the audit's first rows
show best-ask size 2 991 vs. best-bid 155 — a 20:1 asymmetry); spread that is highly
persistent (ACF 0.516) and quantised at a few values; mid changes clustered at dealer
re-quote times rather than at trade times.

The data are consistent with **M2, M3, or a mix**, and *inconsistent with M1 as the
dominant mechanism*. This is the single most consequential inference in either document,
and it has not been tested with the one join that would settle it.

### 5.2 Implications, in order of importance

**(a) The efficient-price innovation \(\Delta P_t\) is *not generated on this venue*.**
Under M2, \(\Delta P\) is \(\Delta M^{\text{ref}}\) plus local noise. Its entropy is the
entropy of a *different market* — one on which we have no order-flow information at all
and which our actions cannot influence. Term (A) of §2.1 for the price factor is then
mostly *imported*, and no amount of local-book modelling reduces it. What local modelling
*can* reduce is the entropy of \(D_t\) — the venue's tracking error — which is a
stationary, fast, bounded process and hence *far* more predictable than \(\Delta M\).
The right decomposition of the price factor is therefore
\[
\Delta M_t = \Delta M^{\text{ref}}_t + \Delta D_t,
\qquad
Q(\Delta M_t \mid \mathcal{F}_t) = Q_{\text{ref}}(\Delta M^{\text{ref}}_t)\ast Q_{\text{local}}(\Delta D_t\mid D_{t-1}, \text{book}_t),
\]
with the first factor essentially a heavy-tailed martingale we *accept*, and the second
a mean-reverting process we *model*. Everything in the roadmap about Student-t
innovations and volatility tracking applies to the first factor; the second is where the
market maker's edge lives.

**(b) The market maker is not competing with informed traders; it is competing with
the anchor.** Under M1, adverse selection comes from traders who know where \(P\) is
going. Under M2/M3, adverse selection comes from *being slower than the anchor to
re-quote when the reference moves*. The relevant risk is not "who is on the other side of
this trade" but "how stale is my quote relative to the reference right now" — a latency
and feed-quality problem, not an information-asymmetry problem. Fill probability
conditional on \(D_t\) is then the key object: a fill that arrives when \(|D_t|\) is
large and *moving away* is toxic; one that arrives when \(D_t\) is near zero is spread
capture.

**(c) Zero forward flow → price information is *good news* for the maker.** If flow did
predict price, then every fill would carry information against us (the Glosten–Milgrom
tax). That it does not means fills are, to first order, uninformed — retail liquidity
demand — and the spread is earned rather than lost. The 0.171 backward correlation is the
*mechanical* cost of that liquidity provision (the book walks when we are hit) and reverts
by lag 2. This is the economic content of "flow ⊥ next-mid": **it says the venue's
liquidity takers are, on average, not informed about the reference.** A maker on such a
venue should price inventory risk from the reference's volatility and price adverse
selection from its own staleness, and should *not* widen quotes in response to local
flow imbalance.

**(d) Under M3, the dealer's re-quote process is the price process.** If one participant
sets the mid, then \(\Delta M_t\) is that participant's *decision*, and its law is a policy
— responsive to their inventory, their reference, and their risk appetite — not a
market aggregate. A policy is far more structured than a market: it has a finite set of
re-quote triggers, a characteristic step size (quantised mid changes), and a reaction
time. That structure is *learnable* in a way that a many-agent aggregate is not. The test
is whether \(\Delta M\) is concentrated on a few multiples of the tick, whether re-quotes
cluster in time, and whether the best-quote sizes are stable across re-quotes (same
participant re-posting).

> **Test 5.2.a (decisive).** With the Postgres reference feed: (i) fit \(D_t\) as
> AR(1); report its half-life and stationary sd. (ii) Regress \(\Delta M_t\) on
> \(\Delta M^{\text{ref}}_t\), \(\Delta M^{\text{ref}}_{t-1}\), \(D_{t-1}\), net-flow\(_{t-1}\).
> Under M2 the reference coefficients are ≈1 and \(D_{t-1}\) is strongly negative;
> net-flow\(_{t-1}\) loses significance once \(D_{t-1}\) is present. (iii) Report
> the prequential NLL of the price factor with and without these regressors. That single
> number decides whether the local venue is a market or an echo — and thus which of the
> two documents' modelling programmes applies.

### 5.3 Reference pricing, price discovery, and where the entropy actually comes from

If M2 holds, "price discovery" on this venue is a misnomer: the venue *imports* discovery
and *adds* microstructure noise. The information-theoretic picture is a channel: the
reference process is the source, the venue is a noisy channel with delay, and the mid is
the output. Channel delay is the maker's opportunity (quote ahead of the venue's
adjustment) and channel noise is the maker's risk (the venue's mid can deviate from the
reference for longer than we can hold inventory).

Two questions become answerable: *how much delay* (the lag at which
\(\mathrm{corr}(\Delta M^{\text{ref}}_{t-k},\Delta M_t)\) peaks) and *how much noise*
(the stationary sd of \(D_t\)). Both are properties of the venue's dealer population and
will drift with it — N5 in §3.5 — which is why a detector for "the venue's tracking
behaviour has changed" belongs in the lab.

If instead M1 holds — the local venue genuinely discovers price, e.g. during hours when
the reference is closed or during a local-currency shock — then the backward
correlation is real price adjustment, the forward one *should* appear at finer time
scales, and the roadmap's programme is the right one. The two regimes may alternate:
echo during liquid reference hours, discovery during reference outages. That alternation
is itself a regime variable and a cheap one to condition on (reference-feed staleness).

## 6. What the market maker actually needs from the prediction layer

### 6.1 Less variance uncertainty at the same directional uncertainty

The user asked: *could a model with lower variance uncertainty but the same directional
uncertainty help?* Yes — and the mechanism is precise. Let the maker's one-step P&L on a
quote pair at half-spread \(\delta\) with fill probabilities \(p^\pm(\delta)\) be
\[
\pi = \delta\,(F^+ + F^-) - \Delta P\,(F^+ - F^- + I),
\]
with \(F^\pm\) fill indicators and \(I\) inventory. Its expectation depends on
\(\mathbb{E}[\Delta P\mid\mathcal{F}_t]\) (direction) only through the inventory term, and
that expectation is ≈ 0 by the martingale property. Its *variance* — and hence any
risk-adjusted objective, and hence the optimal \(\delta\) and the optimal inventory skew —
depends on \(\mathrm{Var}(\Delta P\mid\mathcal{F}_t) = \sigma_t^2\). The Avellaneda–Stoikov
reservation-price and spread formulas are explicit in \(\sigma_t\); a maker who knows
\(\sigma_t\) to 20 % rather than 100 % sets a spread that is closer to optimal by a
corresponding margin *every step*, with no directional view at all. §2.3's ranking
(volatility channel worth 0.2–0.4 nats vs. mean channel 0.03) is the same statement in
score units. A prediction layer that delivers a well-calibrated \(\sigma_t\) path and an
honest tail is therefore worth more to the controller than one that shaves the mean.

### 6.2 The objects the controller consumes, and the scores that match them

The MPC does not consume \(Q(\Delta M)\). It consumes, per candidate action:

| Controller object | Prediction-layer input | Score that matches |
|---|---|---|
| fill probability \(p^\pm(\delta, q)\) at distance \(\delta\), queue \(q\) | flow intensity by side, conditional on book & \(D_t\) | log score of the fill *indicator*, stratified by \(\delta\) |
| markout \(\Delta P_{t+k}\) after a fill | \(\sigma_t\) path, tail, \(D_t\) mean reversion | CRPS / quantile loss at \(k\in\{1,5,20\}\), *conditional on fill* |
| inventory-risk over horizon \(h\) | joint law of \(\{\Delta P_{t+j}\}\) — vol *persistence* matters | variogram / energy score on the path |
| adverse-selection flag | \(P(|D_{t+1}| > \text{threshold}\mid \mathcal{F}_t)\) | Brier / log score on the exceedance |

Two remarks. First, several of these are *conditional on our own action*, which isTwo remarks. First, several of these are *conditional on our own action*, which is
exactly the interventional gap the roadmap flagged (§3.2 there). A fill-probability model
trained on observational data where we quoted at \(\delta\) tells us nothing about
\(\delta' \neq \delta\) unless the model has structure (monotone in \(\delta\), queue-
position aware) that lets it extrapolate. Second, the marginal NLL on \(\Delta M\) appears
nowhere in the table. It is a *diagnostic* for the innovation law and the volatility
filter; it is not the objective. The lab should report the table's scores alongside NLL,
and a model that improves NLL while worsening the conditional markout score should be
treated as a regression.

### 6.3 Acting on the process: the entropy that depends on the action

§2.5 noted that \(H(X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_t,\mathrm{do}(A_t))\) is a function of the action.
Under M2/M3 this has a concrete form. If we quote *inside* the dealer's spread, we become
the best quote, our fills rise, and the mid — defined from best quotes — now moves when
*we* re-quote. We have not predicted the mid; we have partly *become* it. The observed
\(\Delta M\) series then contains our own policy, the flow → price backward correlation
now measures *our* book-walking, and any model fitted to it is fitting a closed loop.

This is not a pathology to avoid; it is the regime the maker is trying to reach. But it
has a consequence for the lab: **data collected while the bot was quoting aggressively
and data collected while it was passive are draws from different processes**, and the
`run_id` / action columns are not optional metadata — they are the conditioning variable
that keeps the two apart. A model lab that pools them is estimating a mixture of the
venue-with-us and the venue-without-us, weighted by how often we were active. The
minimal fix is to record our own quote state on the 30 s grid and condition on it; the
principled fix is to treat prediction and control as one problem, which is what an RL
formulation would do — and why RL is the correct *framing* even where it is the wrong
*estimator* (too little data, too non-stationary a reward, too dangerous to explore live).

### 6.4 A note on RL and on Transformers, from this vantage

Both are asked about; both get the same answer for different reasons.

*RL* is the right objective (the policy's expected risk-adjusted P&L under the
interventional law) and the wrong training procedure for this data volume. The
decomposition that makes it tractable is exactly the one above: a *prediction layer*
that is scored prequentially on observational objects, and a *policy layer* that is
optimised offline against the prediction layer's samples (model-based RL / MPC). The
prediction layer's job is to be calibrated *where the policy will drive the system*, which
is why stratified scores in §6.2 matter more than pooled NLL.

*Transformers* are attention over long contexts. §3.3 measured the useful context: tens
of minutes for the scale state, one or two buckets for flow, essentially zero for the
mean beyond lag 1. There is no long-range structure for attention to find on this venue
at this resolution — unless the reference feed is included, in which case the structure
is a *known* lead–lag relation with one or two parameters, not a learned one. The
appropriate use of a large model here is not as the predictor but as an *anomaly
detector* for N5: trained on the venue's normal joint behaviour, its reconstruction error
is a plausible "the mechanism has changed" signal. That is a different role and a
different score.

Questions 1 and 8 are the two that can invalidate everything downstream, and neither can
be answered from the CSV. That is the honest summary of this document: **the four-day
extract has told us what it can; the next unit of progress is a join, not a model.**

### 7.1 What would make this document wrong

- If Test 5.2.a finds \(D_t\) non-stationary or the reference coefficients far from 1, the
  venue is a genuine discoverer and §5's economics inverts: fills *are* informative,
  flow imbalance *should* widen quotes, and the roadmap's flow-conditioned price model
  is right.
- If Test 1.2.a leaves excess kurtosis ≫ 0 after standardisation *and* Test 5 finds the
  scale state well tracked, there is a real heavy-tailed innovation on this venue and the
  Student-t prescription stands unmodified.
- If the half-life sweep has a sharp interior minimum and BOCPD run-lengths are long,
  the process is N2-only, the mixture architecture is unnecessary, and EWMA with the
  right half-life is near-optimal — the simplest outcome and not an unlikely one.

Each of these would be a *finding*, and a good one. The purpose of a theoretical
companion is not to be right about the venue but to make the venue's answer legible.
