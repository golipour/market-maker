## 1. Formal Problem Statement

### 1.1 The Stochastic Process

Let the complete market state at interval index \(t\) be:

\[
\mathbf{x}_t = \bigl(V_t^{\mathrm{buy}},\, V_t^{\mathrm{sell}},\, M_t,\, S_t,\, \mathcal{B}_t\bigr)
\]

where \(V_t^{\mathrm{buy}}, V_t^{\mathrm{sell}} \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\) are aggressive volumes aggregated over the interval of width \(\Delta\) (e.g. 30 s), \(M_t = \frac{1}{2}(\text{best\_bid}_t + \text{best\_ask}_t)\) is the mid price, \(S_t\) is the spread, and \(\mathcal{B}_t\) is the full limit-orderbook snapshot (a random measure on price space).

We work on a filtered probability space \((\Omega,\, \mathcal{F},\, (\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \geq 0},\, \mathbb{P})\) where \(\mathcal{F}_t = \sigma(\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_t)\) is the natural filtration generated by the process.

### 1.2 The Prediction Problem

At every step \(t\), given the observed history \(\mathbf{x}_{0:t}\), we wish to maintain a predictive probability measure \(Q_t\) over \(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\). Define:

\[
Q_t(\cdot) = Q(\cdot \mid \mathbf{x}_{0:t},\, \theta_t)
\]

where \(\theta_t\) are parameters learned from \(\mathbf{x}_{0:t}\) and must **not** depend on \(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\). This is the no-look-ahead constraint that matches production.

The per-step loss is the negative log-likelihood:

\[
\ell_t = -\log Q_t(\mathbf{x}_{t+1})
\]

and the aggregate evaluation metric is:

\[
\overline{\ell}_T = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \ell_t
\]

This is **prequential** or **sequential** log-loss, first axiomatised in Dawid (1984).

### 1.3 Factored vs. Joint Prediction

The current system factorises the predictive distribution:

\[
Q_t(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}) = Q_t^{\mathrm{flow}}\bigl(V_{t+1}^{\mathrm{buy}}, V_{t+1}^{\mathrm{sell}}\bigr)
\;\cdot\;
Q_t^{\mathrm{price}}\bigl(\mathbf{p}_{t+1} \mid V_{t+1}^{\mathrm{buy}}, V_{t+1}^{\mathrm{sell}}\bigr)
\]

where \(\mathbf{p}_{t+1}\) is the price-related state (mid, spread, book shape). This factorisation is **exact** if and only if

\[
V_{t+1} \perp_{\mathbb{P}} \mathbf{p}_{t+1} \;\Big|\; \mathcal{F}_t,
\]

i.e., flow and price moves are conditionally independent given history. This is a modelling assumption that may or may not hold; testing it is part of the empirical agenda (§9).

Furthermore, the flow model currently factorises the two sides:

\[
Q_t^{\mathrm{flow}}\bigl(V^{\mathrm{buy}}, V^{\mathrm{sell}}\bigr) = Q_t^{\mathrm{buy}}\bigl(V^{\mathrm{buy}}\bigr) \cdot Q_t^{\mathrm{sell}}\bigl(V^{\mathrm{sell}}\bigr)
\]

This is an additional conditional independence assumption: buy and sell flow are independent given history. Given microstructure evidence of order-flow correlation (informed traders can hit both sides sequentially), this should also be tested.

### 1.4 The Online Protocol

The required online protocol (matching production) is:

```
θ₀ ← initial parameters (prior)
for t = 1, 2, …, T:
    ℓₜ ← -log Q(xₜ | x₀:ₜ₋₁, θₜ₋₁)   # score with PRE-UPDATE params
    θₜ ← Update(θₜ₋₁, xₜ₋₁, xₜ)      # update AFTER scoring
report (1/T) Σ ℓₜ
```

This is strictly causal. There is no offline pre-training phase in production; but there can be an **initialisation phase** where the model runs silently on a warm-up window before it begins scoring.

### 1.5 What Is Not Being Modelled Here

To keep scope clear: the model lab is purely about producing calibrated predictive distributions over the next interval state. The **Bellman equation for the MPC**, bid/ask ladder optimisation, PnL, fill probability, and inventory risk are all downstream of the model and are not the objective here. However, the coupling between model quality and MPC quality is discussed in §5.4.

## 2. Information-Theoretic Foundations

Before trying any model, we should ask: *what is the theoretical minimum loss possible for any predictor on this process?*

### 2.1 The Entropy Rate: The Fundamental Lower Bound

For a stationary ergodic process \((\mathbf{x}_t)\), the **entropy rate** is:

\[
h \;=\; \lim_{t \to \infty} H\bigl(\mathbf{x}_{t+1} \mid \mathbf{x}_{0:t}\bigr)
\;=\; \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{T} H(\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_{T-1})
\]

**No predictive model can achieve per-step NLL below \(h\), regardless of complexity.** This is the irreducible uncertainty of the process — the entropy of the true conditional distribution.

By Dawid's prequential consistency theorem: if \(Q_t\) is produced by a well-calibrated Bayesian predictor under the correct model class, then:

\[
\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \ell_t \;\xrightarrow{a.s.}\; h
\]

This means the gap \(\overline{\ell}_T - h\) is what any improvement in modelling can hope to recover. The quantity \(h\) is not directly observable, but it can be estimated (§9.2).

**This gap is the most important number in the entire research programme.** If the current models are already close to \(h\), there is little room for improvement and no complex model will help. If the gap is large, there is structural predictability being left on the table.

### 2.2 Decomposing the Total Entropy

Under the factored model:

\[
H(\mathbf{x}_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t)
= \underbrace{H(V_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t)}_{\text{flow entropy}}
+ \underbrace{H(\mathbf{p}_{t+1} \mid V_{t+1}, \mathcal{F}_t)}_{\text{price entropy | flow}}
+ \underbrace{I(V_{t+1};\, \mathbf{p}_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t)}_{\text{factorisation error}}
\]

The third term is zero only under the conditional independence assumption from §1.3. If the two sub-processes are not conditionally independent, the factored model **cannot** achieve \(h\) even with perfect component models.

### 2.3 Predictive Information and Feature Value

Define the **predictive information** that feature set \(\mathcal{I}_t \subseteq \mathcal{F}_t\) carries about the next observation:

\[
\mathcal{PI}(\mathbf{x}_{t+1} ; \mathcal{I}_t) = H(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}) - H(\mathbf{x}_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{I}_t)
\]

This is the mutual information \(I(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}; \mathcal{I}_t)\). It answers the question: *how much does knowing this feature set reduce the uncertainty about the next state?*

Key quantities to estimate (§9.3):

- \(I(\mathbf{x}_{t+1};\, M_t)\) — value of last mid price alone
- \(I(\mathbf{x}_{t+1};\, V_t)\) — value of last-interval volume
- \(I(\mathbf{x}_{t+1};\, \mathcal{B}_t)\) — value of full orderbook snapshot
- \(I(\mathbf{x}_{t+1};\, \mathbf{x}_{t-k:t})\) — value of a lag-\(k\) history window
- \(I(\mathbf{x}_{t+1};\, \tau_t)\) — value of time-of-day (a deterministic feature)

A feature with near-zero mutual information with the target should not be in the model; a feature with high mutual information relative to the total \(h\) is where modelling effort should focus.

### 2.4 Transfer Entropy and Causal Structure

The **transfer entropy** from the volume process to the price process:

\[
\mathrm{TE}_{V \to M}(k) = I\bigl(M_{t+1};\, V_{t-k+1:t} \;\big|\; M_{t-k+1:t}\bigr)
\]

measures how much predictive information about future mid price is carried by volume history, over and above mid price history alone. This is the information-theoretic version of Granger causality and its estimation should precede all price modelling decisions.

Similarly, \(\mathrm{TE}_{M \to V}\) measures reverse causality (price changes predicting future flow), which would argue for joint modelling of \((V, M)\).

## 3. Non-Stationarity: Types, Implications, and Diagnostics

The process \((\mathbf{x}_t)\) is almost certainly **not stationary** in the strict sense. This is not a technicality — it fundamentally governs which model classes are theoretically justified.

### 3.1 A Taxonomy of Non-Stationarity

We distinguish the following types, each with different theoretical implications:

**Type I — Trend non-stationarity (integrated processes).** The mid price \(M_t\) is in all likelihood an \(I(1)\) process: it has a unit root and is non-stationary in level. Returns \(\Delta M_t = M_t - M_{t-1}\) are likely \(I(0)\). This is the **standard microstructure assumption** and means: any model predicting price levels is misspecified; models should predict **increments or residuals**, not levels. The current production models do this correctly (they model \(\Delta \text{mid}\), residuals, etc.).

**Type II — Covariance non-stationarity (time-varying second moments).** Even if returns are \(I(0)\), their variance (and higher moments) may be time-varying: \(\mathrm{Var}(\Delta M_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}) = \sigma_t^2\) where \(\sigma_t^2\) is itself a stochastic process. This is the ARCH/GARCH family of models. Empirically, this is very common in crypto; volatility clusters and bursts. The 48h data summary (std of mid ~700 IRT) is a marginal number — the **conditional** std is what matters and it almost certainly varies more than the marginal suggests.

**Type III — Structural breaks (abrupt regime shifts).** Occasionally, the entire data-generating process changes: a market stress event, a new market maker entering, an exchange rule change. These are abrupt, not gradual. Standard EWMA forgetting does not handle these well — forgetting is proportional to time elapsed, but a structural break requires forgetting all prior parameters immediately. This motivates online change-point detection (§8.5).

**Type IV — Locally stationary processes (slow drift).** The most common form: the process is approximately stationary on a local time scale \(T_{\text{local}}\), but the local distribution drifts slowly as a function of calendar time. Dahlhaus (1996) formalises this as **locally stationary processes**, where the spectral density is a smooth function of rescaled time. EWMA-type forgetting is the correct first-order approximation for this type.

**Type V — Periodic non-stationarity (seasonality).** Time-of-day and day-of-week effects. A 30-second crypto market on Nobitex will behave very differently at 2:00 AM Tehran time vs. 2:00 PM. This is deterministic non-stationarity and is therefore the easiest to handle: include calendar time as an explicit conditioning variable.

### 3.2 Implications for Model Choice

| Non-stationarity type | Correct first-order treatment | Model class |
|---|---|---|
| Type I (unit root in price) | Model increments/residuals, not levels | Already done in production |
| Type II (time-varying vol) | Adaptive variance; GARCH-type | EWMA on variance; GARCH; ARCH neural |
| Type III (structural breaks) | Reset parameters at detected breaks | BOCD; particle filter with jump |
| Type IV (slow drift) | Exponential forgetting with optimal \(\gamma\) | EWMA with optimal decay |
| Type V (periodicity) | Condition on calendar features | Any model with time-of-day covariates |

A model that handles only Type IV but operates in a market with frequent Type III breaks will have periodic catastrophic NLL spikes.

### 3.3 Forgetting Factor Theory

Consider a sequence of parameters \(\theta_t^* = \theta_{t-1}^* + \xi_t\) where \(\xi_t\) is i.i.d. drift noise with variance \(\tau^2\). For an exponential family model with sufficient statistic \(T(x)\), the EWMA with decay \(\gamma\) produces an estimate \(\hat\theta_t = (1-\gamma)\sum_{s \leq t} \gamma^{t-s} T(x_s)\). The mean-squared tracking error is minimised at:

\[
\gamma^* = 1 - \sqrt{\frac{\tau^2}{\sigma_{\theta}^2}}
\]

where \(\sigma_\theta^2\) is the observation variance of the sufficient statistic. **This is why the optimal decay is a data-dependent quantity**, not a hyperparameter to tune over a grid. If \(\tau^2 = 0\) (perfectly stationary), \(\gamma^* \to 1\) (no forgetting). If the process changes rapidly, \(\gamma^* \to 0\) (fast forgetting). The rate of non-stationarity \(\tau^2\) can be estimated directly from the data (§9.1), giving a principled initialisation of the decay.

## 4. Predictability Analysis

### 4.1 The Central Question

The observation that \(|\rho(M_t, V_t)| < 0.05\) does **not** mean the process is unpredictable. Pearson correlation measures only linear, same-time-step, marginal dependence. The relevant objects are:

1. **Serial autocorrelation**: Is \(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\) correlated with past \(\mathbf{x}_{t-k}\)?
2. **Nonlinear dependence**: Is there a function \(f(\mathbf{x}_{t-k:t})\) that predicts \(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\) better than the unconditional mean?
3. **Conditional heteroskedasticity**: Is the **conditional variance** of \(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\) predictable, even if the conditional mean is not?

Case 3 is particularly important: even if mid price returns are a martingale difference sequence (unpredictable in mean), the variance of those returns may be highly predictable (ARCH effects). A model that correctly forecasts the distribution's **spread** while centering on zero still beats a model that assigns fixed variance.

### 4.2 Martingale Hypothesis for Mid Price

The random walk / efficient market hypothesis for mid price states:

\[
\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = M_t
\]

i.e., price changes have zero conditional mean. The **Variance Ratio Test** (Lo & MacKinlay, 1988) tests this directly:

\[
\mathrm{VR}(k) = \frac{\mathrm{Var}(\Delta_k M_t)}{k \cdot \mathrm{Var}(\Delta_1 M_t)}
\]

- \(\mathrm{VR}(k) = 1\): consistent with random walk (unpredictable mean)
- \(\mathrm{VR}(k) < 1\): mean-reverting (microstructure bounce; bid-ask bounce)
- \(\mathrm{VR}(k) > 1\): momentum (trending)

For illiquid crypto pairs like USDT_IRT with wide spreads, the bid-ask bounce induces negative autocorrelation at lag 1, so \(\mathrm{VR}(2) < 1\) is expected. The **microprice** (Roll-adjusted mid) removes this effect. Both should be tested.

**Critical implication**: if mid price returns are a martingale difference sequence after correcting for bid-ask bounce, then no model can predict the mean of \(\Delta M_{t+1}\) better than zero. The entire value of the price model comes from predicting the **distribution shape** (variance, tail behaviour), not the mean.

### 4.3 Predictability of the Zero-Flow Indicator

With 59% zero-volume intervals, the Bernoulli indicator \(I_t = \mathbf{1}[V_t > 0]\) is particularly important. Define the conditional probability of activity:

\[
p_{1,t} = \mathbb{P}(I_{t+1} = 1 \mid \mathcal{F}_t)
\]

If \(p_{1,t} \equiv 0.41\) (the unconditional rate) regardless of history, then the zero-indicator is unpredictable and the current hurdle approach is optimal for the indicator component. But if the durations between active intervals cluster (a Hawkes-process-like behaviour, which is common in financial markets), then \(p_{1,t}\) is time-varying and predictable. This is testable via:

- **Autocorrelation of \(I_t\)**: if \(\mathrm{ACF}_I(k) > 0\), activity clusters.
- **Duration modelling**: fit an ACD (Autoregressive Conditional Duration) model to inter-trade durations.
- **Conditional entropy of \(I_{t+1}\) given \(I_{t-k:t}\)** vs. \(H(I_{t+1})\): a decrease confirms predictability.

### 4.4 Permutation Entropy

For a univariate series \((x_t)\), the **permutation entropy of order \(m\)** (Bandt & Pompe, 2002) is:

\[
h_{\mathrm{perm}}(m) = -\sum_{\pi \in S_m} \hat{p}(\pi) \log \hat{p}(\pi)
\]

where \(\hat{p}(\pi)\) is the empirical frequency of ordinal pattern \(\pi\) in length-\(m\) windows of the series, and \(S_m\) is the set of all \(m!\) permutations. The **normalised permutation entropy** is \(H_{\mathrm{perm}}(m) = h_{\mathrm{perm}}(m) / \log m!\). Values near 1 indicate near-random (hard to predict in any sense); values significantly below 1 indicate ordinal structure (predictable up to transformations).

This is a robust, nonparametric, model-free measure of predictability, sensitive to nonlinear dependence. It should be computed for \(m \in \{3,4,5,6\}\) for both volume and mid-price series. If \(H_{\mathrm{perm}} > 0.95\) for all features at all lags, the data is very close to white noise and significant NLL improvement over a naive model is unlikely regardless of model complexity.

### 4.5 What Predictability Means for Model Scope

| Diagnostic outcome | Implication |
|---|---|
| \(I_t\) is clustered (ACF > 0) | Hawkes / ACD duration model for zero indicator is theoretically motivated |
| Mid returns are martingale difference | Price model should focus on variance/distribution shape, not mean |
| Mid returns show momentum (\(\mathrm{VR}(k) > 1\)) | AR or linear conditional mean components have value |
| Mid returns show mean-reversion (\(\mathrm{VR}(k) < 1\)) | Roll-spread adjustment; OU process around fair value |
| Permutation entropy near 1 for all features | Gap \(\overline{\ell}_T - h\) is small; no complex model will help much |
| Strong time-of-day in any feature | Add calendar covariates; mixture-of-expert models indexed by time-of-day |
| Transfer entropy \(\mathrm{TE}_{V \to M} \gg 0\) | Volume is causally informative for price; joint model is worth the cost |

## 5. The Prequential Objective and Its Justification

### 5.1 Why Negative Log-Likelihood?

NLL is not an arbitrary choice. It belongs to the family of **proper scoring rules**: a scoring rule \(S(Q, x)\) is proper if \(\mathbb{E}_P[S(Q, x)] \leq \mathbb{E}_P[S(P, x)]\) for all \(Q \neq P\), with equality iff \(Q = P\). Logarithmic scoring (NLL) is the unique proper scoring rule that is also **local** (the score at \(x\) depends only on the probability assigned to \(x\), not to other events) by a theorem of Bernardo (1979).

**Calibration via properness**: if NLL is minimised, the model \(Q\) must asymptotically match the true conditional distribution \(P\). Any other loss function (MSE, MAE, quantile loss at a single quantile) can be minimised by a model that is systematically miscalibrated.

**KL divergence connection**:

\[
\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T -\log Q_{t-1}(\mathbf{x}_t)
= h + \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \mathrm{KL}\bigl(P(\cdot \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}) \;\|\; Q_{t-1}(\cdot)\bigr) + o(1)
\]

Minimising mean NLL is exactly minimising average KL divergence from the true conditional distribution. This is the information-theoretically correct objective for generative model selection.

**Minimum Description Length (MDL) interpretation**: by the Shannon source coding theorem, \(-\log Q_{t-1}(\mathbf{x}_t)\) is the number of nats required to encode \(\mathbf{x}_t\) using the predictive distribution \(Q_{t-1}\). Total NLL = total codelength of the sequence under the model. Minimising NLL = finding the most compact description = Occam's razor made precise.

### 5.2 Necessary Caveats about NLL

Despite its theoretical centrality, NLL has practical failure modes that must be acknowledged:

**Sensitivity to tails.** A single step where the model assigns near-zero probability catastrophically dominates the mean NLL. If the tail behaviour of the model is wrong (e.g., Gaussian tails for a heavy-tailed distribution), rare events inflate NLL by a large factor. This is not just a statistical artefact — it reflects genuine model failure. But it makes mean NLL sensitive to outliers in ways that can obscure typical performance.

*Mitigation*: always report both mean NLL (primary) and median NLL (robust). A model with lower mean but higher median NLL may simply have better tail coverage of rare events, which is meaningful. Large divergences between mean and median are diagnostic of tail misspecification.

**Aggregation over regimes.** Mean NLL over a 48h window aggregates performance across potentially very different regimes (high-volume, quiet, trending, mean-reverting). A model that excels in one regime but fails in another may have the same mean NLL as a mediocre model in all regimes. Per-regime NLL decomposition (§9.6) is necessary to understand this.

**Misalignment with MPC objective.** The MPC uses the model to sample **paths** for a multi-step rollout and then optimises bid/ask placement over those paths. The quality of the rollout samples depends on the **joint distribution** over multiple steps, not just the one-step predictive distribution scored by NLL. Two models with the same one-step NLL can produce dramatically different rollout distributions.

This misalignment is irreducible in the current framework. The correct resolution is to evaluate models by their **downstream MPC performance in simulation** (a much more expensive but more aligned objective). The model lab's NLL is a necessary but not sufficient proxy. Models that win strongly on NLL should be validated by inserting them into the MPC simulator and measuring PnL on historical rollouts.

### 5.3 Alternative Proper Scoring Rules

If tail sensitivity is a practical concern, the following proper scoring rules are worth considering as supplementary metrics:

**Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS)** for scalar targets:

\[
\mathrm{CRPS}(Q, x) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \bigl(F_Q(y) - \mathbf{1}[y \geq x]\bigr)^2 dy
\]

where \(F_Q\) is the CDF of \(Q\). CRPS is proper and strictly less sensitive to tail errors than NLL. For the volume model, CRPS on \(\mathbb{E}[V \mid \theta]\) is more interpretable than NLL.

**Energy score** (Gneiting & Raftery, 2007): a proper multivariate scoring rule that generalises CRPS to the joint prediction of \((V^{\mathrm{buy}}, V^{\mathrm{sell}})\) or the full \(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\).

**Quantile-weighted CRPS**: placing more weight on upper quantiles if what matters to the MPC is large-volume events (which consume depth most aggressively).

### 5.4 The MPC Coupling Principle

Even if NLL is accepted as the primary metric, the coupling between predictive accuracy and MPC quality is not automatic. It holds under a specific **sufficiency condition**: the generative model \(Q\) is a **sufficient statistic** for the MPC's decision problem, meaning no additional information about future market state is extractable from the market beyond what \(Q\) already encodes. If this holds, better NLL → better MPC. If the MPC's rollout objective is sensitive to specific features of the joint distribution not captured by the one-step \(Q\), the coupling can break.

**Practical implication**: when comparing two candidate models on the leaderboard, the one with lower mean NLL should be preferred, but any model that wins by \(\Delta \overline{\ell} < 0.01\) nats and is more computationally expensive should be validated end-to-end before deployment.

## 6. Online Learning Theory: The Correct Frame

The problem is fundamentally an **online learning** problem, not a batch ML problem. This distinction is not cosmetic — it governs which theoretical tools are appropriate and what guarantees can be made.

### 6.1 The Regret Framework

In online learning, the agent produces a sequence of predictions \(Q_0, Q_1, \ldots\) and the environment reveals observations \(\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2, \ldots\) adversarially (or stochastically). The **static regret** against a reference class \(\mathcal{Q}\) is:

\[
\mathrm{Regret}_T(\mathcal{Q}) = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \ell_t - \min_{Q \in \mathcal{Q}} \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} -\log Q(\mathbf{x}_{t+1})
\]

The static regret measures excess loss over the best fixed model in hindsight. For any algorithm in the exponential family, the Bayesian universal predictor achieves \(\mathrm{Regret}_T = O(\log T)\), which is optimal (Cover, 1991).

However, static regret is the **wrong objective for a non-stationary market**. We want to track a time-varying optimum \(\theta_t^*\) — the best model for the current local regime. The relevant object is **tracking regret** (also called dynamic regret):

\[
\mathrm{TrackReg}_T = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \ell_t - \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \min_{\theta} -\log P(\mathbf{x}_{t+1} \mid \theta_t^*)
\]

where \(\theta_t^*\) is the locally optimal parameter at time \(t\). If the parameter path \(\{\theta_t^*\}\) has total variation \(C_T = \sum_t \|\theta_t^* - \theta_{t-1}^*\|_2\), then the **optimal tracking regret** scales as \(O(T^{2/3} C_T^{1/3})\) for general online convex optimisation (Besbes, Gur & Zeevi, 2015).

**This means**: for a highly non-stationary market (\(C_T\) large), even the theoretically optimal online algorithm has unavoidably large tracking regret. Improving model architecture yields diminishing returns once the model is adaptive enough to track the drift rate.

### 6.2 The Forgetting Mechanism as Regret Minimisation

EWMA-type forgetting is a specific form of **Gradient Descent with exponential weighting** on the loss. For the log-loss objective on an exponential family model:

\[
\hat\theta_{t+1} = \arg\max_\theta \sum_{s \leq t} \gamma^{t-s} \log P(\mathbf{x}_s \mid \theta)
\]

This is a **sliding window maximum likelihood** with exponential weighting. The EWMA update rules in the current models are the closed-form solution to this optimisation for exponential family models with conjugate priors.

**Optimal \(\gamma\) from drift rate**: as shown in §3.3, the optimal decay is determined by the ratio of drift variance to observation variance. Sweeping \(\gamma\) on a grid is an empirical heuristic; the theoretically principled approach is to **estimate the drift rate from data** and compute \(\gamma^*\) directly.

### 6.3 Bayesian Online Learning: The Gold Standard

Pure Bayesian prediction is optimal in the following sense: if the true data-generating process is in the model class \(\mathcal{Q}\), then the Bayesian posterior predictive achieves:

\[
\sum_{t=1}^T -\log Q_t^{\mathrm{Bayes}}(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}) \leq \sum_{t=1}^T -\log P(\mathbf{x}_{t+1} \mid \theta^*) + \log \frac{1}{P(\theta^*)}
\]

The excess is the prior codelength \(\log(1/P(\theta^*))\) — a constant independent of \(T\). This is Bayesian optimality: the regret against the true parameter is \(O(1)\), not \(O(\log T)\) for the sequential Bayes predictor.

**In the non-stationary case**, pure Bayes fails because the posterior concentrates at a fixed \(\theta^*\) and cannot track a drifting process. Extensions:
- **Mixture-of-experts Bayes**: maintain a distribution over possible drift models
- **Bayesian change-point detection** (Adams & MacKay, 2007): maintain a distribution over run-lengths since the last change, with each run having its own local posterior
- **Particle filter**: approximate non-stationary Bayesian filtering by a weighted particle ensemble

### 6.4 The Expert Aggregation Principle

Given \(K\) component predictive models \(\{Q^{(1)}, \ldots, Q^{(K)}\}\), the **Hedge algorithm** (Freund & Schapire, 1997; equivalent to Vovk's aggregating algorithm, 1990) maintains weights:

\[
w_t^{(i)} \propto \exp\!\left(\eta \sum_{s < t} \log Q_s^{(i)}(\mathbf{x}_{s+1})\right)
\]

and predicts:

\[
Q_t = \sum_i w_t^{(i)} Q_t^{(i)}
\]

The regret guarantee is:

\[
\frac{1}{T} \mathrm{Regret}_T \leq \frac{\log K}{2T\eta} + \frac{\eta}{8}
\]

Optimised at \(\eta = \sqrt{8 \log K / T}\), this gives \(\overline{\ell}_T \leq \min_i \overline{\ell}_T^{(i)} + \sqrt{\frac{\log K}{2T}}\).

**This is a theoretically guaranteed improvement over any individual model in the ensemble.** The ensemble NLL can never be worse than the best component model by more than \(O(T^{-1/2})\). Applied to the current model catalogue (all hurdle variants, both price models, baselines), this gives a strong and nearly free baseline to beat.

Furthermore, the weights \(w_t^{(i)}\) dynamically track which model is performing best at each point in time — providing automatic regime-based model selection without explicit regime detection.

## 7. What the Current Models Are Actually Doing

### 7.1 Hurdle-Lognormal as Sufficient Statistics Tracking

The current flow model maintains:

\[
\hat\theta_t = (p_0^t,\, \mu^t,\, \sigma^t)
\]

The EWMA updates are the closed-form maximisers of the exponentially weighted log-likelihood for a hurdle-lognormal model:

\[
\hat\theta_t = \arg\max_\theta \sum_{s \leq t} \gamma^{t-s} \log P_{\mathrm{hurdle\text{-}LN}}(V_s \mid \theta)
\]

This is exact for the lognormal family because its sufficient statistics are \((\mathbf{1}[V=0], \log V \cdot \mathbf{1}[V>0], (\log V)^2 \cdot \mathbf{1}[V>0])\), and EWMA is exact for sufficient statistics in exponential families.

**What this cannot capture:**
- Dependence of \(P(V=0)\) on past history (it only tracks the running average zero-rate)
- Conditional heteroskedasticity: if \(\sigma_t^2 = \sigma^2(V_{t-1})\) (ARCH-in-volume), the static EWMA sigma is an average over all regimes
- Multi-modality: if there are two distinct flow regimes (quiet / active), the lognormal mixture is misspecified; a single lognormal is pulled toward a weighted average of two modes
- Cross-side dependence: the independence of buy and sell sides means it cannot capture correlated bursts

### 7.2 Conditional Mechanical Mixture as Regime Gating

The conditional mixture model is more interesting theoretically. It defines a **latent discrete variable** \(r_t \in \{\mathrm{market}, \mathrm{refresh}\}\) and mixes:

\[
Q_t(\Delta\mathbf{p}_{t+1}) = q_{\mathrm{market}}(V_{t+1}) \cdot P_{\mathrm{market}}(\Delta\mathbf{p}_{t+1}) + q_{\mathrm{refresh}}(V_{t+1}) \cdot P_{\mathrm{refresh}}(\Delta\mathbf{p}_{t+1})
\]

where the gate \(q_{\mathrm{market}}\) is a sigmoid function of total volume. This is a **continuous mixture** (not an HMM), where the regime probability is an explicit function of an observed covariate.

The finding that \(\mathrm{corr}(V_{t+1},\, q_{\mathrm{market}}) \approx -0.42\) is theoretically significant: high-volume intervals are more likely to be classified as **refresh** (not flow-driven), which is counterintuitive. This may reflect that on this particular venue (Nobitex USDT_IRT with limited depth), large market orders immediately deplete the book and trigger mass limit order refreshes, making the realised VWAP path look like a refresh event even when a large market order hit.

**What this cannot capture:**
- Non-linear mixing: the sigmoid gate is a linear logistic regression on log(1 + volume); the true switching mechanism may be more complex
- Asymmetric gates: the same gate is used for buy and sell; buy and sell flow may have different mechanical impact coefficients
- Temporal dependence in regimes: the model has no memory of which regime the previous step was in (it is not an HMM)

### 7.3 What "Beating the Baseline" Actually Means

The baselines are theoretically meaningful:

- `baseline:zero_flow` assigns all mass to \(V=0\). Its NLL at each step is \(-\log P(V=0 \mid \theta_{\text{empirical}})\). If the true zero-rate is \(p_0 = 0.59\), then `zero_flow` achieves NLL \(\approx -\log 0.59 \approx 0.527\) per zero step and \(\infty\) per non-zero step. Mean NLL over all steps is driven to \(+\infty\) by the 41% non-zero steps. So `zero_flow` always loses on mean NLL (not median NLL). This is correct: assigning zero probability to events that occur is catastrophic.

- `baseline:mid_random_walk` assigns a Gaussian to \(\Delta M_{t+1}\) with EWMA-estimated mean and variance. This is the **diffusion model** for price, which is the minimum structural assumption consistent with the efficient market hypothesis. Beating this baseline on price NLL means the model captures distributional features beyond a drifting Gaussian, not necessarily that it predicts the direction of price moves.

## 8. Theoretically Organised Model Landscape

This section organises all possible modelling approaches by their **theoretical properties** rather than by implementation. The progression is from simpler to more expressive, with each layer only justified when the simpler one is demonstrably inadequate.

### 8.1 Layer 0: Exponential Family with Exact Sufficient Statistics (Current Baseline)

**Class**: any model where the sufficient statistics have closed-form EWMA updates.  
**Examples**: Gaussian, Gamma, Lognormal, Weibull, Negative Binomial, their hurdle extensions.  
**Theoretical justification**: optimal for stationary exponential family processes; approximately optimal for slowly drifting processes.  
**Limitations**: fixed functional form; independence of sides; no serial dependence; single regime.

**Research question for this layer**: is the exponential family assumption correct? Does the true conditional distribution of \(V_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\) belong to any exponential family? The answer comes from the diagnostics in §9.4.

### 8.2 Layer 1: Bayesian Sequential Updating with Conjugate Priors

Rather than EWMA, use exact Bayesian updating. For a Gamma-distributed volume with shape \(\alpha\) and rate \(\beta\):

\[
P(\alpha, \beta \mid V_{1:t}) \propto P(\alpha, \beta) \cdot \prod_{s=1}^t \mathrm{Gamma}(V_s;\, \alpha,\, \beta)
\]

This is not conjugate in closed form for Gamma (only for Poisson and certain others), but:
- For the **hurdle Bernoulli component** (\(I_t \sim \mathrm{Bern}(p)\)), the Beta prior is conjugate and gives exact online Bayesian updating:
  \[
  p \mid I_{1:t} \sim \mathrm{Beta}\!\left(\alpha_0 + \sum_s I_s,\; \beta_0 + t - \sum_s I_s\right)
  \]
  The predictive probability is the Beta-Binomial, and this is **strictly better than EWMA** in terms of uncertainty quantification.
- For the continuous component: Gaussian with unknown mean and variance → Normal-Inverse-Gamma conjugate, giving a Student-t predictive.

**Advantage over EWMA**: the Bayesian update naturally quantifies **uncertainty about parameters** (posterior width) and propagates that into the predictive distribution. EWMA produces point estimates and then plugs them in, under-estimating predictive uncertainty in regimes with few observations.

### 8.3 Layer 2: Forgetting Bayesian Updates (for Non-Stationarity)

Combine Bayesian updating with forgetting. The **Discounted Bayesian** approach raises the posterior to a power \(\gamma < 1\) at each step:

\[
P_t(\theta) \propto P_{t-1}(\theta)^\gamma \cdot P(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \theta)
\]

This is the continuous-time limit of the EWMA and, for exponential families, reduces to exactly the EWMA update on sufficient statistics. For non-conjugate families, it is approximated via Sequential Monte Carlo.

The **Gaussian process** (GP) over model parameters provides a principled non-parametric prior for how parameters drift over time. A GP with a Matérn covariance in time gives a temporal smoothness prior. But GPs scale poorly with sequence length; sparse GP approximations are needed for online use.

### 8.4 Layer 3: Regime-Switching Models (Hidden Markov Models)

Introduce a discrete latent regime \(r_t \in \{1, \ldots, K\}\) with Markov dynamics:

\[
P(r_t = j \mid r_{t-1} = i) = A_{ij}
\]

Each regime has its own emission distribution \(P(\mathbf{x}_t \mid r_t = k, \theta^{(k)})\). The forward algorithm gives the exact online posterior:

\[
\alpha_t(j) = P(r_t = j \mid \mathbf{x}_{1:t}) \propto P(\mathbf{x}_t \mid r_t = j)\sum_i A_{ij} \alpha_{t-1}(i)
\]

and the one-step predictive distribution is:

\[
Q_t(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}) = \sum_j \left[\sum_i \alpha_t(i) A_{ij}\right] P(\mathbf{x}_{t+1} \mid r_{t+1}=j, \theta^{(j)})
\]

**Advantages**:
- Explicitly models discrete regime structure (quiet vs. active market)
- Transition matrix \(A\) learns the persistence of regimes
- Automatically down-weights past observations in the wrong regime

**Limitations**:
- Fixed number of regimes \(K\) must be pre-specified (or selected by NLL on validation data)
- Parameters \(\theta^{(k)}\) are assumed stationary within each regime
- Online EM for parameter updates is approximate (Cappé & Moulines, 2009)

For the flow model, \(K=2\) (quiet / active) is a natural starting point motivated by the observed bimodality of volume distributions.

### 8.5 Layer 4: Bayesian Online Change-Point Detection (BOCD)

Adams & MacKay (2007) introduced a principled framework for handling abrupt non-stationarity without pre-specifying the number of regimes. Maintain a posterior over the **run length** \(r_t\) (time since last change point):

\[
P(r_t, \mathbf{x}_{1:t}) = \sum_{r_{t-1}} P(r_t \mid r_{t-1}) \cdot P(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-r_t:t-1}) \cdot P(r_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})
\]

At each step, the model either extends the current run (\(r_t = r_{t-1} + 1\)) with probability \(1 - H\) (hazard rate), or restarts (\(r_t = 0\)) with probability \(H\). After a restart, the model begins fitting fresh from the new regime's data. The predictive distribution marginalises over all possible run lengths:

\[
Q_t(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}) = \sum_{r_t} P(r_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{1:t}) \cdot Q(\mathbf{x}_{t+1} \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-r_t:t})
\]

**Advantage**: handles structural breaks of unknown frequency without model selection for \(K\). The hazard rate \(H\) is the single hyperparameter controlling expected regime duration.

**Disadvantage**: assumes that after a change point, all prior information is discarded. In reality, the market may shift in one parameter but be stable in others.

### 8.6 Layer 5: Neural Probabilistic Models

Neural approaches become justified when the conditional distribution of \(\mathbf{x}_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t\) is provably non-exponential-family and requires a universal function approximator. The key constraint is: **the model must produce calibrated probability distributions, not just point predictions**.

**Mixture Density Networks (MDN)**:

\[
Q_\theta(\mathbf{x} \mid h_t) = \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k(h_t) \cdot \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu_k(h_t), \sigma_k^2(h_t)\right)
\]

where \(h_t = \mathrm{RNN}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{0:t})\) is a recurrent hidden state. The MDN is trained to minimise NLL on the training sequence. At inference, \(h_t\) is updated by one forward pass per step — \(O(1)\) in model dimension.

**Normalising Flows** (Rezende & Mohamed, 2015): learn an invertible transformation \(f_\theta : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d\) such that \(f_\theta^{-1}(\mathbf{x})\) has a simple base distribution (e.g. standard Gaussian). The exact density is:

\[
\log Q_\theta(\mathbf{x} \mid h_t) = \log p_{\mathrm{base}}\!\left(f_\theta^{-1}(\mathbf{x})\right) + \log \left|\det \frac{\partial f_\theta^{-1}}{\partial \mathbf{x}}\right|
\]

Normalising flows can represent any continuous distribution to arbitrary precision but are computationally expensive.

**The Online Learning Problem for Neural Networks**: standard SGD on new data causes **catastrophic forgetting** — the network forgets the distribution of older data as it fits newer observations. For our protocol, this is a fundamental issue:

- One new observation arrives every 30 seconds.
- A single gradient step per observation is the only feasible update.
- After \(T\) updates, the effective training set contains roughly \(1/(1-\gamma) = n_{\text{eff}}\) samples with exponential weighting.
- Standard neural networks with millions of parameters are severely overparameterised for \(n_{\text{eff}} \sim 20\text{–}100\) effective samples.

Principled mitigations:
- **Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC)**: add a penalty \(\lambda \sum_i F_i (\theta_i - \theta_i^*)^2\) where \(F_i\) is the Fisher information of parameter \(i\) under the old posterior. This slows forgetting in directions of high importance.
- **Reservoir Sampling**: maintain a fixed-size buffer of past observations sampled uniformly; retrain periodically on buffer + recent data.
- **Hypernetworks**: a meta-network generates the parameters of the predictive model conditioned on recent history — the "model update" is done by the hypernetwork in a forward pass rather than gradient descent.
- **Low-rank adaptation**: freeze most parameters (trained offline on diverse market data) and update only a low-rank residual per market session.

### 8.7 Layer 6: Transformers as In-Context Online Learners

A sufficiently large transformer trained on diverse time series can perform **in-context learning**: given a context window \((\mathbf{x}_{t-L:t})\) as input, it performs implicit Bayesian inference about the distribution of \(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\) without any gradient update.

Theoretical basis (Garg et al., 2022; Akyürek et al., 2022): transformers trained on sequences from a class of distributions \(\mathcal{P}\) learn to implement approximate Bayesian predictors for \(\mathcal{P}\) in their forward pass. The implicit algorithm implemented in the residual stream approximates stochastic gradient descent on the in-context data.

**For our use case**, this suggests two distinct approaches:

**Approach A (pre-trained universal model)**: use a foundation model (e.g. Chronos, TimesFM, Lag-Llama) trained on a broad corpus of financial time series. Run inference with a sliding context window of length \(L\). No gradient updates needed per step. The model adapts automatically within the context window.

*Limitations*: pre-trained on general time series; microstructure features of a specific crypto pair on a specific exchange are likely out-of-distribution. Calibration is often poor. Context window is fixed (no longer-range memory). Computational cost is \(O(L^2)\) per step in attention, which at 30-second intervals may be acceptable.

**Approach B (domain-trained small transformer)**: train a small transformer (4–8 layers, \(d_{\text{model}} = 64\text{–}128\)) offline on all available historical intervals, then deploy with sliding-window inference. Periodic offline retraining (daily/weekly) on accumulated data replaces online parameter updates. The key design choice is the **output head**: it must produce a parametric distribution (MDN with \(K\) components, or quantile outputs) to be scoreable by NLL.

This avoids the catastrophic forgetting problem entirely: the model is never updated online. The non-stationarity is handled by the context window (recent history in the window represents the current regime) and by offline retraining frequency.

### 8.8 Layer 7: Reinforcement Learning — The Correct Scope

RL optimises a cumulative reward signal. It is **not the correct framework for the prediction sub-problem** in the model lab. However, RL is relevant at two other points in the system:

1. **Model-based RL (MBRL) perspective**: the MPC is a model-based planner, and the generative model is the "world model." The full system (world model + MPC) is an instance of MBRL. The correct evaluation of the world model in this context is by the **downstream policy performance**, not by one-step NLL. This creates a bilevel structure: lower-level (NLL optimisation) is what the model lab does; upper-level (PnL optimisation through MPC) is what eventually matters.

2. **Adaptive inventory management**: if the market maker's inventory position is a state variable, the full problem is a POMDP (partially observed MDP) with a dynamic market. RL could learn a policy that jointly chooses model updates and bid/ask placement. This is a research frontier, not a near-term practical option.

**Conclusion**: the model lab's objective (NLL) is correct for the world-model sub-problem. RL is the right frame for the MPC layer, not the prediction layer.

### 8.9 Summary Comparison

The table below organises approaches by theoretical properties, not by name:

| Layer | Model class | Non-stationarity handling | Tail flexibility | Cross-feature dependence | Online cost per step |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | EWMA / exponential family | EWMA decay (implicit) | Fixed family | None (factored) | \(O(d)\) |
| 1 | Conjugate Bayes | None (concentrating posterior) | Fixed family | None | \(O(d)\) |
| 2 | Discounted Bayes / SMC | Forgetting weight / particle jitter | Any family | Possible | \(O(N \cdot d)\) |
| 3 | HMM | Regime switching (Markov) | Per-regime family | Via joint emission | \(O(K^2 \cdot d)\) |
| 4 | BOCD | Detected change-points | Per-regime family | Via joint emission | \(O(T_{\max} \cdot d)\) |
| 5 | Neural MDN / flows | EWC / reservoir / hypernetworks | Universal | Full \(\mathbf{x}\) jointly | \(O(d_{\text{net}})\) per forward pass |
| 6 | Transformer (in-context) | Context window | Universal | Full \(\mathbf{x}\) jointly | \(O(L^2 \cdot d_{\text{model}})\) |

## 9. Prescribed Empirical Investigation (Pre-Modelling)

**None of this analysis requires choosing or fitting a model.** It characterises the data directly and answers the theoretical questions raised above. Each test is linked to a specific theoretical implication. The Postgres tables `mm_events` and `mm_market_facts` contain all needed data.

### 9.1 Non-Stationarity Characterisation

**Test**: Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) and KPSS on:
- `mid` (expect unit root)
- `delta_mid = mid_t - mid_{t-1}` (expect stationarity)
- `log(1 + buy_volume)`, `log(1 + sell_volume)` conditional on V > 0
- `buy_volume == 0` indicator (Bernoulli; test for parameter stationarity via rolling mean)

**Test**: Rolling variance of `delta_mid` over 1h windows across the full dataset. If rolling variance shows significant temporal variation (e.g., factor of 10 between low and high), ARCH-type conditional heteroskedasticity is present and justifies Layer 2+ models.

**Test**: Chow test for structural breaks at specific calendar times (market open, close, holiday). If present, calendar-conditional models are motivated.

**Estimation**: For EWMA optimal decay (§3.3), estimate:
\[
\hat\tau^2 = \mathrm{Var}(\hat\mu_t - \hat\mu_{t-1})
\]
where \(\hat\mu_t\) is a local parameter estimate. This gives the theoretical \(\gamma^*\) without grid search.

### 9.2 Entropy Rate Estimation

**LZ-based entropy rate** (Ziv & Lempel, 1978): quantise the sequence \(\Delta M_t\) into bins of width \(\epsilon\) (e.g., one tick). Run LZ compression (zlib or bzip2) on the quantised symbol sequence. The compression ratio is a consistent (but biased) estimator of the entropy rate \(h\).

**\(k\)-nearest neighbour entropy estimator** (Kozachenko-Leonenko): for continuous series, estimate \(H(\mathbf{x}_{t+1} \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-L:t})\) via:

\[
\hat H = \frac{d}{n} \sum_i \log \frac{r_{k,i}}{\text{const}} + \log n - \psi(k)
\]

where \(r_{k,i}\) is the distance to the \(k\)-th nearest neighbour of the \(i\)-th delay-embedded vector, and \(\psi\) is the digamma function.

**Target**: compare the estimated entropy rate \(\hat h\) against the current model's mean NLL. If \(\overline{\ell}_T - \hat h < 0.05\) nats, the current model is near-optimal and no further modelling is needed. If the gap is \(> 0.5\) nats, substantial structural predictability remains unexploited.

### 9.3 Feature Information Content

**Mutual information estimation** (using a \(k\)-NN or kernel estimator):
- \(\hat I(V_{t+1}^{\mathrm{buy}}; I_t)\) — does last step's activity predict next step's activity?
- \(\hat I(\Delta M_{t+1}; \mathcal{B}_t)\) — does orderbook shape predict price moves?
- \(\hat I(\Delta M_{t+1}; \Delta M_{t-k})\) for \(k = 1, 2, 5, 10\) — autocorrelation in price changes
- \(\hat I(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}; \tau_t)\) — how much is explained by time-of-day

**Transfer entropy** \(\hat{\mathrm{TE}}_{V \to M}\): run a conditional MI estimator on the delay-embedded series. If this is significant, price and flow should not be modelled independently.

### 9.4 Distribution Tail Behaviour

For the positive-volume observations:
- **Hill estimator** for the tail index \(\xi\): \(\hat\xi = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^k \log(V_{(n-i+1)}/V_{(n-k)})\) where \(V_{(j)}\) are order statistics. If \(\xi > 0\): heavy-tailed (power law); if \(\xi \approx 0\): exponential; if \(\xi < 0\): bounded tail. This directly informs the family choice (lognormal, Pareto, Weibull).
- **QQ-plots** against lognormal, Gamma, Weibull to visually assess fit quality.
- **Excess kurtosis of log-volume**: Gaussian assumption valid only if kurtosis \(\approx 3\).

For mid-price changes:
- **Shapiro-Wilk** or **Jarque-Bera** on \(\Delta M_t\): is the Gaussian assumption for the residual model valid?
- **Tail quantiles**: compute empirical P90, P95, P99, P99.9 of \(|\Delta M_t|\) and compare against normal/t-distribution predictions.

### 9.5 Autocorrelation Structure

Compute full ACF and PACF for:
- \(\Delta M_t\) (mid price changes)
- \((\Delta M_t)^2\) and \(|\Delta M_t|\) (volatility proxies): if ACF decays slowly, ARCH effects are present
- \(I_t = \mathbf{1}[V_t > 0]\) (activity indicator)
- \(\log V_t \cdot I_t\) (log-volume of active intervals)
- \(I_t \cdot I_{t-1}\) (joint activity): tests for activity clustering

**Ljung-Box test** for joint significance of ACF at lags 1–20 for each series.

**If ACF(\(I_t, k\)) > 0 for small \(k\)**: activity clusters → Hawkes or ACD process is motivated.  
**If ACF(\((\Delta M_t)^2, k\)) > 0 for small \(k\)**: volatility clusters → GARCH or adaptive-variance model is motivated.  
**If all ACFs are consistent with white noise**: the process has no serial dependence and no time-series model (beyond a marginal distribution) can improve prediction.

### 9.6 Regime Identification (Unsupervised)

Fit a two-state Gaussian HMM to \((\Delta M_t, \log(1+V_t))\) jointly (using a batch EM offline). Examine:
- What are the two regimes? (high-vol/high-flow vs. low-vol/low-flow)
- What is the regime persistence matrix \(A\)? If diagonal entries are near 0.99, regimes last ~100 intervals = 50 minutes. If near 0.9, regimes last ~10 intervals = 5 minutes.
- Does NLL under the HMM (on a holdout window) beat NLL under the single-regime model? If yes by \(\Delta > 0.1\) nats, regime-switching models are theoretically justified.

This is a **model-free characterisation** of the regime structure; the fitted HMM is not a production model, just a diagnostic.

### 9.7 Conditional Independence Tests

Test the assumed independence \(V_t^{\mathrm{buy}} \perp V_t^{\mathrm{sell}} \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\):
- Compute \(\hat\rho = \mathrm{corr}(I_t^{\mathrm{buy}}, I_t^{\mathrm{sell}})\): if positive, both sides are active simultaneously (informed order flow hitting both sides); if negative, informed flow is one-sided.
- Kernel Independence Test (HSIC) between \(V_t^{\mathrm{buy}}\) and \(V_t^{\mathrm{sell}}\): a nonlinear test of independence.

Test \(V_{t+1} \perp \mathbf{p}_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t\):
- Regress \(\Delta M_{t+1}\) on \(V_{t+1}^{\mathrm{buy}}\) and \(V_{t+1}^{\mathrm{sell}}\): is the flow-price contemporaneous correlation significant (beyond what the mechanical model captures)?

### 9.8 Calibration Assessment of Current Models

Run the current production models prequentially on the available data and assess:

**Probability Integral Transform (PIT) histogram**: for a scalar observation \(x\) and predictive CDF \(F_t\), compute \(u_t = F_t(x_t)\). Under a correctly specified model, \(u_t \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)\). If the histogram shows U-shape: tails underestimated. If it shows hump-shape: tails overestimated. If it shows monotone slope: bias in mean.

**Empirical coverage of prediction intervals**: for each \(\alpha \in \{0.5, 0.8, 0.9, 0.95\}\), compute the fraction of observations falling within the \(\alpha\)-credible interval of the predictive distribution. This should equal \(\alpha\); deviations indicate miscalibration.

**Regime-conditional NLL**: split steps by quantile of total volume \(V_t^{\mathrm{buy}} + V_t^{\mathrm{sell}}\). Compute mean NLL separately for bottom 25%, middle 50%, top 25%. If the current model is well-calibrated in quiet periods but catastrophically wrong in high-volume periods, a regime-aware model is needed.

## 10. Research Programme and Decision Tree

The empirical investigations in §9 answer binary questions that determine which modelling layers (§8) are theoretically warranted. Here is the decision structure:

```
Step 0 (§9.1, 9.2):  Estimate entropy rate h and non-stationarity type.
│
├─ If (mean_NLL_current - h) < 0.05 nats:
│   STOP. Current model is near-optimal. Focus on MPC calibration, not better models.
│
└─ If gap > 0.05 nats → proceed to Step 1
│
Step 1 (§9.5):  Is there serial autocorrelation in any feature?
│
├─ No serial dependence anywhere (all ACF consistent with white noise):
│   The process is unpredictable beyond its marginal distribution.
│   Only distributional shape (Layer 0/1) can improve NLL.
│   → Investigate tail family only (Layer 0).
│
└─ Serial dependence found → Step 2
│
Step 2 (§9.5):  Is dependence in mean (ACF of Δ M_t) or in variance (ACF of (ΔM)²)?
│
├─ Only in variance (ARCH effects):
│   Model conditional variance; mean is a martingale.
│   → Adaptive sigma EWMA (Layer 0 with separate sigma update).
│   → GARCH-type extension of current models (still Layer 0 family).
│
├─ In mean AND variance:
│   → Layer 3 (HMM for regime) or Layer 5 (neural MDN) may help.
│
Step 3 (§9.6):  Is there regime structure?
│
├─ HMM NLL improves by > 0.1 nats:
│   → Layer 3 (online HMM) is motivated.
│
├─ HMM wins only slightly and BIC prefers 1 regime:
│   → Use Hedge ensemble (§6.4) over current model variants instead.
│
Step 4 (§9.3):  Does transfer entropy TE_{V→M} > 0 significantly?
│
├─ Yes:
│   → Joint flow-price model is motivated; the factored model loses information.
│   → Design the joint distribution Q_t(V_{t+1}, p_{t+1}).
│
├─ No:
│   → Factored model is information-theoretically justified; stay with the two-model structure.
│
Step 5 (§9.4):  Are tails heavier than the current exponential family assumption?
│
├─ Hill estimator suggests power-law tail (ξ > 0.2):
│   → Pareto or truncated Pareto for the positive-volume component.
│   → Student-t innovations for price residuals (already partially in production via student_df param).
│
├─ Tails consistent with lognormal / Gamma:
│   → Family choice is approximately correct; focus on serial dependence, not tail.
│
Step 6 (§9.3):  Does time-of-day carry significant MI with the target?
│
├─ Yes:
│   → Add calendar features to the gate function of the mixture model.
│   → Or: fit separate static parameter initialisations per time-of-day bucket.
│
Step 7: Only if all above warrant it — consider neural / transformer approaches.
   → Justified when: gap remains > 0.3 nats after Layers 0–4; serial nonlinear dependence
     detected by BDS/permutation entropy; and sufficient data volume (> 10k intervals) exists.
```

### 10.1 The Theoretically Strongest Near-Term Intervention

Given the current state of knowledge, the single most theoretically justified near-term improvement — requiring no new architecture — is the **Hedge ensemble** (§6.4) over all current model variants. It has a provable regret guarantee, costs \(O(K)\) per step, and will dynamically weight the best-performing model at each regime. This should be implemented first and constitutes the new baseline.

The second most justified intervention is **estimating the optimal decay \(\gamma^*\) from data** rather than sweeping it on a grid, using the drift-rate estimator from §9.1. This turns the most important hyperparameter into a derived quantity.

### 10.2 Theoretical Conditions for Neural/Transformer Models

A deep model is not theoretically warranted unless **all** of the following hold:

1. The gap \(\overline{\ell}_T - \hat h\) is large (> 0.3 nats) after optimising Layer 0–3 models.
2. The BDS statistic or permutation entropy tests show significant **nonlinear** serial dependence not captured by any exponential family.
3. Sufficient data volume exists for stable offline training (\(> 10^4\) intervals = ~83 hours at 30s).
4. A calibration validation protocol (PIT test, coverage test) is in place before any neural model is used in production.

If these conditions are met, the **domain-trained small Transformer with MDN output head and sliding-window inference** (Layer 6, Approach B) is theoretically preferred over an online-trained RNN because it avoids catastrophic forgetting while still exploiting nonlinear sequential dependencies in the context window.
